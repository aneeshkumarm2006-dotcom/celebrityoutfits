Section 01

How the archive is built

Everything on the public site is assembled from five kinds of record. Understand how they hang together and the rest of this guide is obvious.
CelebrityA person
→
LookOne outfit, one occasion
→
ItemOne garment in it
→
ProductWhere to buy it

A Celebrity is a person we cover. A Look is one outfit they wore on one occasion, and it gets its own page and its own web address. An Item is a single garment inside that look: the jacket, the shoes, the brooch. A Product is a thing a reader can actually buy, which an item points at. Brands sit alongside as a directory, and Articles are the journal: longer writing that is not tied to one outfit.

The reason a look gets its own page rather than being a section of the celebrity page is search: people search for an outfit, not only for a person. A page with a title, a photograph and forty words gives search engines nothing to index, which is why the story field in section 05 matters more than it looks.

Celebrity/celebrities/nameCelebrity index, homepage grid
Look/celebrities/name/lookThe celebrity’s page, homepage latest looks
ItemNo page of its ownListed on the look page it belongs to

Worth knowing early

Items are not created inside a look. You create them separately and point each one at its look. This trips up everybody once. Section 06 explains why it is built that way and how to do it.

Two habits that will save you

  • Your work saves itself. Drafts autosave every few tenths of a second. You will not lose anything by navigating away.
  • Saving is not publishing. A record stays a draft until you publish it. Nothing you type appears on the public site until then. See section 13.

Section 03

Publishing a look, start to finish

This is the job. If you read one section, read this one. The detail behind each step is in the sections that follow.

  1. Check the celebrity already exists

    Open Archive → Celebrities and search. If they are not there, create them first (section 04). A look cannot be saved without a celebrity attached.

  2. Get the photograph, and get it licensed

    Before anything else. An unlicensed photograph is the one mistake on this site that costs real money. Section 08 sets out which sources are allowed.

  3. Upload the photograph to the Library

    Library → Media → Create. Fill in the description, the credit and the licence. The system will not let you save without all three. Set the focal point so the crop keeps the face in frame.

  4. Create the look

    Archive → Looks → Create. Choose the celebrity, give it a title, set the date, place and occasion, and attach the photograph. Full field list in section 05.

  5. Write the story

    A few hundred words on where it was worn, what the outfit is doing, and why it works. This is what makes the page worth its own address.

  6. Save it as a draft

    You need the look to exist before you can attach garments to it.

  7. Add the garments, one at a time

    Archive → Items → Create, once per garment, each pointing at this look. For every one you must choose a confidence level. That decision is section 06, and it is the most important judgement you will make here.

  8. Attach products where a reader can buy something

    Search the existing Products first; most arrive automatically from merchant feeds. Only create one by hand if it genuinely is not there. Section 07.

  9. Read it back, then publish

    Check every confidence label is one you would defend to the brand’s lawyer. Then publish the look. The public pages update within seconds.

The standard to hold

A reader should never be told something is confirmed when it is a guess. The site’s whole value is that its labels are honest. An open “we could not confirm this” costs us nothing; one invented brand costs us the reason anybody trusts the archive.

Section 04

Adding a celebrity

Archive → Celebrities → Create. Short record, two images, one piece of real writing.
FieldWhat to put in it
NameRequiredAs they are normally credited. “Michael B. Jordan”, not “Jordan, Michael B.”
SlugRequiredThe web address. Fills in automatically from the name. Leave it alone unless you have a reason, and read section 13 before changing one that is already live.
CategoryRequiredFilm & TV, Music, Sport, or Creator. Pick what they are best known for.
StandfirstOne paragraph. Not a biography: a thesis. What is distinctive about how this person dresses? This is the hardest sentence on the record and the most read.
BioLonger background. Optional, and it can wait.
Portrait imageUpright, taller than wide (3:4). Used on cards and lists.
Hero imageWide. Used for the full-width band at the top of their page.
FeaturedPins them above everything else on the index.
RankLower numbers come first among featured people. Ignore it unless you are ordering the front page.

Why two images

A tall portrait cropped into a wide band loses the head; a wide shot squeezed into a card becomes a sliver of shoulder. They are different pictures for different shapes, not the same picture twice. If you only have one, upload it as the portrait and leave the hero empty.

A good standfirst, and a bad one

Weak: “An English actor known for the Spider-Man films and a number of stage roles.”

Strong: “Dresses like someone who knows exactly how much of the reference to leave in. The Spider-Man tours are the clearest case: the colours are always there, the logo never is.”

Section 05

Adding a look

Archive → Looks → Create. One outfit, on one occasion. This is the page the reader lands on from search, so it carries the most weight.
FieldWhat to put in it
CelebrityRequiredWho is wearing it. Must exist already.
TitleRequiredDescribe the outfit, do not name the event. “Crimson Jacquemus, and a navy tie” beats “Spider-Man premiere”. The event has its own field.
SlugRequiredAuto-filled from the title.
DateRequiredWhen it was worn, not when you are writing.
LocationCity, or the venue if it is famous. “London”, “Leicester Square”.
OccasionAirport, Premiere, Press call, On set, Street, or Event.
EventThe specific occasion. “Spider-Man: Brand New Day UK premiere · Odeon Luxe Leicester Square”. Leave empty rather than guess.
DescriptionOne or two sentences on what is being worn. Used on cards and as the search-result snippet, so keep it short and concrete.
StoryThe long form. See below.
PhotosOne or more. The first one is the lead image. Drag to reorder.
ItemsRead-only list. It fills in as you create items pointing at this look.
Featured / RankPinning and ordering, as with celebrities.

What the story is for

Aim for three or four short sections with headings. A shape that works every time:

  • The setting. Where, when, what the occasion was.
  • The outfit. What the pieces are, in order, in plain words.
  • Why it works. The actual argument. This is the part readers come back for.
  • What you can actually buy. Honest about what is bespoke, couture, or long gone.

You can drop a buy module directly into the writing, so a product sits next to the sentence about it rather than only in the list underneath. Use the block menu in the editor.

House style

No em dashes anywhere in reader-facing writing. Use a colon for a gloss, a full stop for a separate thought, brackets for an aside, a comma for a trailing qualifier. This is enforced across the site and it is the fastest way to spot copy that was not written by a person.

Section 06

Identifying garments

Archive → Items → Create, once per garment. Every item carries a confidence level, and that label is the most important thing you will type here.

Why items are created separately

You do not add garments inside the look. You create each one in the Items collection and point it at the look using the Look field in the sidebar. It is built that way so the whole archive can be reviewed at once: “show me every unidentified jacket” is a question worth being able to answer, and it is impossible if items are buried inside individual outfits.

The fields

FieldWhat to put in it
DescriptionThe garment in words a reader recognises. “Plain white crew-neck tee”, not “knitwear”. Without it the page can only show a category the reader can already see.
LookRequiredWhich outfit this belongs to. In the sidebar.
CategoryRequiredOuterwear, knitwear, shirting, tailoring, denim, trousers, footwear, eyewear, watch, bag, jewellery, other.
ConfidenceRequiredSee the table below. Never mark something confirmed to fill a gap.
Evidence noteWhy you made this call. “Logo visible on temple.” Never shown publicly, but it is the audit trail if a brand queries it. Always fill it in.
ProductThe main thing we link to.
Alternative productA cheaper stand-in, shown when the original is over $500 or out of stock.
More optionsFurther ways to buy the same garment. Cheapest first.
PositionDisplay order within the look. Top of the body downwards is a sensible default.

The four confidence levels

LevelUse it whenExample
Confirmed
Logo, hardware or stylist-credited		 You can point at proof: a visible logo, distinctive hardware, or a credit from the stylist or brand. The style name matches a current listing and the brand posted the look themselves.
Closest match
Unbranded in the photo		 You are confident about the house but cannot read a label, or the piece is custom and built off a known collection. Reported as custom Jacquemus off a named collection, but not read off a label.
Get the look
Cheaper equivalent		 You are openly offering something similar rather than the real thing. A high-street coat in the same cut as an unbuyable bespoke one.
Open
Not enough detail to call		 You genuinely cannot tell. This is a perfectly good answer. A plain white shirt with no visible detail.

The one rule that matters

If you are choosing between two levels, choose the lower one. Marking something confirmed because the page looks better with a brand name on it is the only way to genuinely damage this site. Readers forgive “we could not tell”. They do not come back after being sold the wrong coat.

Known gap

There is no “dress” or “gown” category yet, so evening wear currently goes under other. Flag it if this starts getting in your way; it is a small change.

Section 07

Products and buy links

Archive → Products. Most of these arrive automatically from merchant feeds, along with their photography. Search before you create.
FieldWhat to put in it
NameRequiredAs the merchant lists it, but say the colour out loud if it differs from the worn piece. “La Veste Cabri blazer in navy” tells the reader before they click that it is not the crimson one.
BrandLinks to the brand directory.
DescriptionOne line. Cut, colour, material: what distinguishes it.
PriceIn minor units. $171.00 is entered as 17100. This catches everyone once.
CurrencyUSD, CAD, GBP or EUR.
Affiliate URLRequiredThe tracked merchant link.
MerchantWhere it is bought. “Nordstrom”, “Farfetch”.
Image / Image URLUploaded image, or the address of the merchant’s own photograph.
SKUThe merchant’s code. This is what feed updates match on, so do not edit it.
In stockKept up to date automatically for feed products.

Never paste a raw affiliate link into writing

Buy links must always go through a product record. The public site turns them into a tracked redirect of its own, which is how we get click data the same day and how a merchant can be swapped without editing a single article. A raw affiliate link pasted into a story bypasses all of that and silently stops earning if the merchant changes.

Honesty about price and availability

If the exact piece is unbuyable, say so in the writing and link the nearest honest thing, with the difference in the product name. Do not attach a lookalike as though it were the original. Several resellers list “as worn by” garments that turn out to be a different outfit relabelled; check that what you are linking is actually the piece in the photograph.

Section 08

Images and licensing

This is the single largest legal risk to the business, so the system refuses to save an image without a credit and a licence. That is deliberate. Do not look for a way around it.

Every image needs three things

FieldWhat to put in it
Alt textRequiredDescribe the picture for someone who cannot see it, and for search. “Tom Holland at the UK premiere in a burgundy double-breasted suit with a jewelled brooch.”
CaptionOptional line shown under the image.
CreditRequiredPhotographer or agency, worded exactly as the licence demands. “Amy Sussman / Getty Images”.
LicenceRequiredWhich of the five routes below.
Source agencyAppears when the licence is an agency one. Which agency it is with.
Licence expiresLeave empty if it does not expire. Fill it in if it does, or we will still be publishing it after the right has lapsed.

The five permitted routes

LicenceWhat it means
Agency licenceWe pay an agency such as Getty. Record which one.
Official embedThe celebrity’s or brand’s own Instagram or X post, embedded the official way. Free, and permitted by those platforms’ terms.
Creative CommonsFreely licensed, usually from Wikimedia. Credit exactly as the licence requires.
Our ownWe shot or commissioned it.
Brand promotional assetSupplied by a brand’s press office for editorial coverage.
Merchant product feedProduct photography that came with an affiliate feed.

A credit is not a licence

Adding “courtesy of” to a photograph you found online does not give you the right to publish it. Red carpet photographs belong to agencies, and finding a copy somewhere it has been reposted does not change that. If you cannot honestly pick one of the six routes above, you do not have the picture. Publish the look without an image, or do not publish it yet.

Focal point

Each image is automatically cut into eight different shapes for different parts of the site. On a full-length shot, the automatic centre crop will cut the head off in the wide banner. Drag the focal point onto the face and every crop follows it. Takes three seconds and it is the difference between a professional page and a broken one.

Writing an article

Journal → Articles → Create. Longer writing that is not tied to a single outfit.

Pick a template first

The template shapes how the piece is presented and what search engines are told about it. Choose it before you write, not after.

How-to / tutorial“How to wear a fishtail parka”
Listicle“Eight premiere suits worth stealing”
Comparison“Bespoke vs made to measure”
ReviewA single product or service, assessed
News / updateSomething that just happened
Generic articleAnything that does not fit the others

The fields

FieldWhat to put in it
TitleRequiredThe headline.
ExcerptRequiredThe card summary and the search snippet. Write it as a promise the piece keeps.
Hero imageLicensed, as always.
BodyThe writing, with blocks. See below.
Published atThe date shown to readers.
AuthorByline.
Related celebrity / brandCross-links the piece into the archive.

Blocks you can drop into the body

  • Shoppable product — a buy module inline, with a small label above it.
  • Pull quote — a line lifted out, with optional attribution.
  • Image — at text width, wider than the text, or full bleed.
  • Gallery — several images together.
  • Embed — Instagram, YouTube, or X. This is also the licensed way to show a celebrity’s own photograph.
  • Callout — a note, or a “get the look” box.

Keyword links

In the SEO tab you can list keywords with the address each should link to. Set the relationship for each one: leave it on Auto unless you know better. Use Sponsored for anything paid or affiliate, and Nofollow for links you do not want to vouch for. Getting this wrong is a search penalty, not a style question.

The same tab shows live checks on the piece as you write. Work down them until they are clear.

Section 10

The request queue

Requests → Requests. Readers send us outfits to identify through the “Find my outfit” page. Each one is a person waiting for an answer.

Working a request

  1. Open the oldest new request

    They are worked in the order they arrive. There is no queue to jump, and we say so publicly.

  2. Set it to Researching

    So nobody else picks up the same one.

  3. Do the work

    Reverse image search, runway and lookbook archives, stylist credits, the brand’s own catalogue.

  4. Publish the answer as a look or an article

    Then link it back using the resulting look or resulting article field, so the queue records where the answer went.

  5. Set the outcome and email the reader

    Answered, or could not identify. Both are real outcomes. Record the date you told them.

The five statuses

StatusMeans
NewNobody has looked at it.
ResearchingBeing worked on now.
AnsweredIdentified and published, reader told.
Could not identifyGenuinely unidentifiable. Tell them plainly. This is not a failure.
Declined / spamNot a real request.

Reader photographs are private

Images sent in through the form are held separately from the main library and are not public. We look at them to do the research. Never move one into the Library or publish it, whatever it shows, unless we have licensed that image ourselves. We promise this on the form, in writing.

Section 12

Homepage, navigation, footer

Under Settings. These affect every page on the site, so change one thing at a time and look at the result.

Homepage

Built from sections you can switch on and off and reorder: hero, latest looks, celebrity grid, standards, and journal preview. Each has its own heading and settings, and each can be disabled without deleting it.

Site settings

Site name, tagline, default description, logo, and the default sharing image. It also holds two pieces of legally required text:

  • Affiliate disclosure — appears on every page carrying a buy link. Required by law. Do not remove it or water it down.
  • Non-affiliation notice — states that we are not endorsed by the celebrities or brands we cover. Also do not remove it.

Navigation and footer

Lists of links with a label and an address each; the footer is grouped into columns. If you add a link to a page that does not exist, it will 404 for every visitor, so paste addresses rather than typing them.

Section 13

Publishing, drafts and URLs

How work goes live, and the one action that can quietly cost the site traffic.

Draft and published

  • Everything starts as a draft and is invisible to the public.
  • Drafts autosave continuously. You will not lose work.
  • Publish makes it live. The public pages refresh within seconds.
  • You can schedule a publish for a future date, which is how you have a premiere look go live the moment an embargo lifts.
  • The last 20 versions of each record are kept, so you can compare and roll back.

Featured and rank

Featured pins a record above everything else. Rank orders the pinned ones, lower numbers first. If everything is featured, nothing is.

Changing a slug on a live page

The slug is the web address. It fills in from the title automatically, but only while it is empty, so renaming a title never silently moves a published page. If you deliberately change a slug that is already live, every existing link to it and every search ranking it holds will break.

If you must: change it, then immediately add a redirect under Settings → Redirects pointing the old address at the new one. Do not do one without the other.

Section 14

House rules and gotchas

The short list. Most of these are mistakes that are easy to make once and awkward to undo.

Never

  • Mark an item confirmed without evidence you could point at.
  • Publish a photograph that does not fit one of the licence routes in section 08.
  • Publish a reader’s submitted photograph.
  • Paste a raw affiliate link into writing instead of using a product record.
  • Remove the affiliate disclosure or the non-affiliation notice.
  • Change a live slug without adding a redirect.
  • Let a commission rate influence what we identify or how we label it.

Always

  • Fill in the evidence note, even when the answer is obvious. It is the audit trail.
  • Set the focal point on any photograph with a face in it.
  • Say the colour out loud in a product name when it differs from the piece worn.
  • Check whether a product already exists before creating one.
  • Choose the lower confidence level when you are torn between two.
